Diffuse Blocks

Programming for Architecture

Topics in Machine Learning, The Cooper UnionFall 2023
Role
Collaborative project (2 people)
Instructors
Prof. Ben Arenda, Sam Keene
With
Tate Liang

At a moment when AI technologies were only beginning to enter mainstream design discourse, this study explored the emerging role of machine learning in architectural pedagogy and concept development. Before generative tools like Stable Diffusion became widely adopted, the project investigated how image-to-image generation could influence early-stage design thinking. Using simple wooden blocks as spatial primitives, we employed Stable Diffusion to reinterpret physical arrangements into new architectural forms and visual expressions. This analog-to-digital feedback loop positioned AI not merely as a rendering tool, but as a speculative design collaborator capable of suggesting unexpected formal directions.

To deepen engagement and interactivity, we developed a Python-based program that used a webcam to read the configuration of the wooden blocks in real time, letting users participate directly in the design process and turning a passive interface into a dynamic, creative platform. The project demonstrates the potential of AI to enrich architectural workflows while emphasizing the importance of using these tools thoughtfully, balancing computational innovation with design intuition and hands-on experimentation. It reflects a transitional moment in design education, when emerging technologies began to reshape the boundaries of authorship, imagination, and collaboration in architecture.

Architecture

Living Street

Reparative Infrastructure in Flushing

B.Arch Thesis Project, The Cooper Union2024–2025Flushing, Queens, NY
Role
Individual thesis
Instructor
Prof. Guido Zuliani

This project investigates the spatial conditions of informality along the LIRR railway embankment in Flushing, Queens, where large-scale infrastructure both connects regions and fragments local communities. As a physical and symbolic barrier, the embankment imposes a rigid division within the neighborhood: its elevated tracks and fenced edges cut through the urban fabric, producing long stretches of inaccessible, underutilized space. These infrastructural voids, though centrally located, are often dismissed as leftover or undesirable.

Yet within them, a parallel urban life unfolds. Informal street vendors, predominantly elderly Chinese immigrants, adapt to these spatial margins, operating without legal permits, shelter, or access to basic services. Their vending setups speak to both resilience and exclusion: makeshift tables, crates, and carts line the sidewalk edge, often just feet away from the blank infrastructural wall. These practices are central to Flushing’s street life and economy but remain politically invisible, marginalized by a regulatory system that criminalizes informality. The project asks how these infrastructural blind spots might be reimagined, not as voids to be erased, but as civic thresholds where informal economies are not only acknowledged, but spatially sustained.

In response, the project proposes a reparative design strategy that transforms the void beneath the embankment into a linear civic commons: an adaptable, semi-sheltered corridor that supports street vending, community use, and public utilities. Rather than adding new structures on top of or beside the embankment, the design carves directly into its mass, hollowing out a series of vaulted bays between existing structural columns. These incisions create continuity without erasure, offering vendors a protected environment with electricity and water access. Each bay is a flexible zone that accommodates different vending formats while allowing customization and informal appropriation with vendors’ own tools.

The spatial design is paired with “Sidewalk Tactics,” a foldable zine distributed on-site that outlines vendors’ rights, hygiene practices, and spatial protocols. Rather than formalizing informality, the project frames infrastructure as a platform for care, resilience, and redistribution, envisioning public space that supports those who have long been excluded from it.

Microscopic Infrastructures

Tracking Pollen in Chicago

Design IV, The Cooper UnionSpring 2024Chicago, IL
Role
Collaborative project (3 people); credits per image
Instructor
Prof. Nahyun Hwang
With
Zara Zulfigar, Natalia Boumatar

This project investigates the spatial practices between human bodies, pollinators, and urban environments through the lens of airborne pollen. Set in Chicago, a modern city shaped by fragmented green corridors and seasonal extremes, we explored how pollen functions both as a biological necessity and a spatial agent. Historical research into 19th-century “pollen-free” tourism, where curated landscapes were marketed as therapeutic escapes from urban allergens, revealed how environmental control was deeply connected to spatial design and manipulation. Rather than treating pollen as something to be eliminated, we reframed it as a material that could be observed, interpreted, and thoughtfully integrated into urban systems.

Informed by this research, we developed three site-specific interventions: a floating pollen collector on the Chicago River, a pollinator habitat in a vacant lot in the Park Manor community, and a trackside habitat embedded into Chicago’s rail infrastructure. Each responds to ecological and atmospheric conditions, forming a speculative network for sensing, supporting, and coexisting with airborne ecologies. Through mapping, drawing, and spatial installation, the project makes the invisible visible and envisions ecological monitoring not only as a scientific practice but also as a civic and spatial one.

Robin Hood Gardens

Housing Analysis — London

Design III, The Cooper UnionFall 2022London, UK (precedent)
Role
Collaborative analysis (3 people); credits per image
Instructors
Prof. Daisy Ames, Guido Zuliani, Kaitie Lau
With
Julian Yang, Grant Peck

Robin Hood Gardens was a pioneering housing complex in East London, designed by Alison and Peter Smithson and completed in 1972. As a bold experiment in post-war social housing, it embodied the Smithsons’ vision of “streets in the sky,” reimagining elevated walkways and interconnected balconies as communal arteries within the urban fabric. Though demolished in 2017, it remains a key reference point in the discourse on Brutalism and collective living.

Our analysis focused on how the “street in the sky” and the interlocking units shaped communal life. The elevated walkways acted as shared thresholds, spaces suspended between public and private realms that encouraged casual encounters and a sense of neighborhood above ground. The interlocking duplex units allowed for spatial efficiency, acoustic separation, and dual orientations, enhancing both privacy and connectivity. Together, these strategies offered a layered approach to collective housing that merged infrastructural ambition with human-scale interaction.

City of Artists

Housing Analysis — Paris

Design III, The Cooper UnionFall 2022Paris, France (precedent)
Role
Collaborative analysis (3 people); credits per image
Instructors
Prof. Daisy Ames, Guido Zuliani, Kaitie Lau
With
Julian Yang, Grant Peck

Located in Paris, Cité d’Artistes (City of Artists) by Michel Kagan was developed in the 1980s as part of a broader urban planning initiative aimed at revitalizing underutilized areas of the city. Designed for artists and their families, the project includes 38 individual art studios and 12 family apartments, combining residential and creative functions into a unified architectural system.

Our analysis explored how Kagan’s spatial strategies mediate the intersection of domestic life and artistic production. Rooted in a modernist rationalist framework, the design uses clear geometric forms and carefully organized circulation to balance privacy and collectivity. A key focus was the vertical circulation cores and shared access corridors, which intersect individual units and common spaces, creating both functional efficiency and opportunities for social interaction. Double-height volumes within the live-work units allow flexibility for various creative practices, while the rhythmic layout of courtyards and public walkways fosters a strong sense of community.

Space Block Kamishinjo

Housing Analysis — Osaka

Design III, The Cooper UnionSpring 2023Osaka, Japan (precedent)
Role
Analysis
Instructors
Prof. Mersiha Veledar, Daisy Ames, Nader Tehrani

Space Block Kamishinjo, built on the site of a former public bath, sits within a dense and varied urban fabric. As the first built application of the Basic Space Blocks (BSB) theory, the project introduces a modular system of spatial composition based on cubic units, each measuring 2.4 meters per side, that aggregate into complex, interlocking forms. Rather than organizing space through conventional floor plans, the design prioritizes spatial sequencing and inhabitable volumes; functional elements such as kitchens and bathrooms are introduced secondarily.

Our analysis investigated how this system redefines the relationship between structure and use, generating unexpected light paths, unconventional airflows, and a living environment that challenges the homogeneity of typical apartment typologies. The Kamishinjo prototype serves as a critical reference in the ongoing evolution of the Space Block series, influencing later iterations in Hanoi.

Carbon Form Analysis: IKEA

Red Hook, Brooklyn

Environmental Technology, The Cooper UnionSpring 2023Red Hook, Brooklyn, NY
Role
Collaborative analysis (4 people)
Instructor
Prof. Elisa Iturbe
With
Tate Liang, Evan Chiang, Sofia Mercado

As the world’s dominant ready-to-assemble furniture company, IKEA has established itself as an icon beyond the furniture retailer. Through merchandise, in-store restaurants, and designer collaborations, it has seamlessly integrated itself into mass culture. From the parking lot to the showroom and warehouse, IKEA as a big-box store assembles three typologies into a choreographed sequence designed to promote consumption.

Each typology is organized around a different mode of efficiency: the parking lot maximizes area with minimal turns, the showroom maximizes time spent through a wandering maze layout, and the warehouse maximizes speed through clear, indexed paths. Analyzing its urban context, including access to public and private transportation, shows how IKEA acts as a passive carbon form by privileging private cars, reproducing the current energy paradigm through its spatial and economic configuration.

Inflatable Chair

Low-Carbon, Collapsible Furniture

Environmental Technology, The Cooper UnionSpring 2023
Role
Collaborative project (3 people)
Instructor
Prof. Pamela Cabrera
With
Tate Liang, Evan Chiang

Focusing on the carbon footprints of materials, the Inflatable Chair investigates how inflatable structures can serve as a lightweight, collapsible, and sustainable alternative to conventional furniture. Traditional furniture often relies on dense, resource-intensive materials such as foam, fabric, and metal hardware, which contribute significantly to embodied carbon and transport inefficiencies. This project challenges that norm by using air, an ephemeral, weightless material, as the core component of comfort and structure.

The seat and backrest are made from heat-sealed vinyl, forming soft, air-filled cushions that support the body while dramatically reducing material weight. When deflated, these components collapse into a flat, portable sheet, allowing the entire chair to be packed, stored, or shipped with minimal volume and energy use.

The wooden frame uses a system of traditional Chinese-inspired pin joints that emphasize modularity and portability. Each element is joined without glue or screws, so the structure folds into a compact, linear package. The joints are designed for tool-free assembly, letting the frame be set up in a single step; once locked, it transforms from a flexible assembly into a rigid, stable structure capable of supporting the inflatable seat.

Jaú Bus Terminal Column

Scaled Cast-Concrete Reproduction

Building Technologies, The Cooper UnionFall 2022São Paulo (precedent)
Role
Collaborative project (5 people); production animation by Yinuo Liu
Instructor
Prof. Samuel Anderson
With
Riley Howard, Qrin Park, Tate Liang, Tina Chiang

This project is a scaled cast-concrete reproduction of a column from the Jaú Bus Terminal in São Paulo. We analyzed the column’s flaring geometry and structural logic, which transitions from a narrow square base to a wide, branching canopy that distributes load across a large surface.

The casting process involved designing and fabricating a multi-part negative mold from CNC-cut foam, true-to-scale rebar placement, and careful sequencing of the concrete pour, simulating the real-world construction process over two weeks. The final result showcases both the sculptural beauty and the structural capacity of concrete.

Freehand Drawing

Studies in Blackness and Tone

Freehand Drawing, The Cooper UnionFall 2024
Role
Individual
Instructor
Prof. Sue Gussow

This body of work began as a study of master drawings. I closely observed and reinterpreted the techniques of artists such as Käthe Kollwitz, Odilon Redon, and others whose work expresses emotional depth through tonal intensity. I focused on how blackness, both as a material and as a concept, could be constructed through accumulated marks.

In early studies, I examined how shadows were not simply filled areas, but spaces slowly built up through smudging, layering, and erasure. Each technique introduced a different texture, and each texture altered the quality of darkness in the narrative. As I moved into composition, I began thinking of black not as absence, but as weight and presence, pulling focus and creating contrast. I layered different strategies together, often revisiting a single area over and over to create depth across different levels of grey.
